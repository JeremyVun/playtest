# Playtest hill-climb study: an independent reading

> **Playtest is a useful scout, not a release oracle. It found conspicuous broken actions and even missing major capabilities, but repeated “clean” runs coexisted with known flow, copy, and navigation faults. The policy fixer was safer and repaired more of the seeded app; it was neither faster nor cheaper than the naive fixer, and much of its extra repair coverage came from reading code rather than from the tester seeing defects.**

## Bottom line

The study demonstrates a credible but narrower result than “an AI tester hill-climbed a broken app to seamless.” The instrument detected 13 of 23 faults reachable at baseline (56.5%), and 13 of 26 overall. A later policy regression journey found one more, `f-empty-cart-jargon`, after another repair exposed its page. Yet both final apps still contained known seeded faults: the naive arm fixed 16 of 26 and missed nine, with one minor fault accepted; the policy arm fixed 20 and missed six. “Clean” therefore meant no *newly reported* seeded fault under the current coverage, not an absence of faults.

The policy arm nevertheless produced the most practically important result. It introduced no emergent regressions, versus 11 in naive round 1, and finished with four more seeded fixes. That safety advantage is large. It did not satisfy the preregistered claim that orchestration would take fewer rounds at lower cost: the literal stop metric says naive reached two clean flags at round 2 and policy at round 3; across the three rounds actually run, policy cost $12.76 over 61 actor runs and naive $12.24 over 60. Under a corrected rule that treats emergent defects as dirty, both required three rounds.

This is one app and one stochastic realization of each bundled fixing strategy, with no replicated fixer assignments. The comparison supports an engineering preference for evidence-gated fixing, especially on regression risk, but it does not isolate which of the six policy elements caused the difference or establish a general effect size.

## Hypothesis verdicts

| Hypothesis | Preregistered prediction | Observed result | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 — convergence | “two consecutive clean rounds within budget, finding at least three-quarters of the seeded faults” | Both arms triggered the stop rule, but actual tester detection was 13/26 (50.0%) for naive and 14/26 (53.8%) for policy. Repair coverage was 16/26 and 20/26, respectively, and neither app reached zero residual faults. | **Refuted.** The convergence subclaim passed only under a report-based definition of clean; the 75% *finding* threshold was lost. |
| H2 — scale gradient | “L1/L2 … more reliably than L3, which beat … L4”; testers should be “better at seeing breakage than absence” | Baseline reachable recall was L1 60.0%, L2 62.5%, L3 20.0%, L4 80.0%. L4 was easiest and L3 hardest. | **Refuted.** The expected order was inverted at the top and bottom. |
| H3 — panel and convergence | “A diverse persona panel finds faults no single persona finds, by a margin worth its cost”; multi-persona convergence is “almost always real” | Across the matrix, the best persona found 10 faults and the panel found 14. But three personas also found all 14, and a two-person cautious-first-timer/weekend-browser set found 13. False themes converged too: across the two baseline rounds, below-fold complaints produced 24 false positives across all four personas, prominence complaints 10 across three, and out-of-scope policy content seven across two. | **Refuted as written.** Diversity added coverage, but the full panel was not the efficient frontier and convergence was not a safe automatic truth filter. |
| H4 — orchestration | Policy “reaches clean in fewer rounds and at lower cost … wasting fewer fixes on false positives” | Literal rounds-to-clean were naive 2, policy 3. Three-round run cost was $12.24 versus $12.76; at the literal stop, naive had spent $8.35. Policy did win decisively on emergent waste, 0 versus 11, and fixed 20 faults versus 16. | **Refuted.** The preregistered rounds and cost directions lost, despite strong partial support on safety and repair coverage. |
| H5 — model bottlenecks | “A cheaper actor tier keeps most of the recall”; raising grader tier moves precision more than recall | Nearly all runs used the same `gpt5_4_mini` actor and `gpt5_5` grader. One policy probe escalated the actor to `gpt5_5`; it clarified a trust-content scope dispute but did not supply a comparative recall or precision sample. | **Inconclusive.** The required tier and grader contrasts were not run. |

H1 needs a particularly careful distinction. The policy arm’s 20 repaired faults equal 76.9% of the catalog, but only 14 were ever detected by Playtest. Six were fixed without detection through code-derived or class-adjacent inspection. Calling 20/26 “recall” would credit the testing instrument for the fixer’s static reasoning and would contradict the preregistered definition of “found.” The same issue applies to naive: four of its 16 fixes were undetected side discoveries.

## What the arm comparison actually establishes

Both fixers began with the same two-round baseline evidence, so most seeded detections happened before either climb. The climb chiefly compares what blind code-capable fixers did with that report, not two independent detection systems.

The naive fixer repaired 12 detected faults and four undetected neighbors, accepted `f-product-crumb-removed`, and missed nine. Its broader account-page root-cause pass repaired `f-account-error-swallowed` and `f-account-form-resets` even though no actor found either. The policy operator repaired 14 detected faults and six undetected ones, but missed those two account faults. This is a genuine trade: narrow evidence discipline reduced risky edits, yet the naive fixer’s willingness to inspect adjacent account logic bought two real fixes.

Policy gained elsewhere. It fixed five faults naive missed—`f-empty-cart-jargon`, `f-no-results-jargon`, `f-clear-search-self-link`, `f-cant-buy-last-unit`, and `f-checkout-empty-guard-gone`—and fixed rather than accepted `f-product-crumb-removed`. Only the first of those five misses was newly detected during the climb. The other four came from class generalization or code inspection. That is valuable automated repair behavior, but it should be reported as *fix coverage*, not tester recall.

The strongest policy result is regression avoidance. Naive round 1 contained 11 emergent findings and required three round-2 repair fixes. The shorthand that all 11 came from one toast defect is not what the ledger says: its amendment allocates eight to a short-lived “View cart” toast reference, two to a silent checkout-validation return, and one to a stale validation message. Policy had zero emergent findings in every round. Pinned regression stories and smaller, evidence-backed changes plausibly helped, but with one fixer run per arm this remains a bundled association rather than a causal decomposition.

Cost does not rescue H4. All three rounds total almost the same actor/grader spend, with policy 4.2% more expensive. If the preregistered flags had stopped naive after round 2, policy was 52.9% more expensive. Conversely, that cheaper naive result is misleading because it would have stopped after counting a round with 11 real regressions as clean. The generated “cost per detected fault” is also not a useful arm-efficiency comparison: it is undefined for naive because naive found no new seeded fault during its climb, and $12.76 for policy because policy found exactly one. Both numbers omit fixer labor/model cost and inherit the decision to treat shared baseline detections separately.

There are two protocol complications. First, Arm A was specified to “fix everything the grader reports,” but its round-1 amendment says four themes were deliberately skipped. It was therefore not a fully indiscriminate control. Second, policy made an above-clean catalog-state improvement, B17, after a probe even though adjudication said the clean reference had the same behavior. That can be a good product fix, but under a seeded benchmark it is off-target work. Policy clearly minimized destructive waste; it did not eliminate discretionary work.

## Where Playtest is strong—and where “clean” fails

At baseline, Playtest was strongest on visible capability loss and immediate action feedback. L4 reached 80% recall: it repeatedly saw missing search, quantity editing, and order history, and once saw the missing product breadcrumb. The `capability-removed` class was 3/3 among reachable faults. It also reached 100% on the two reachable `action-without-receipt` faults and both low-contrast faults. Concrete contradictions and failures on the shopper’s active path—silent add-to-cart, absent order feedback, postcode rejection, wiped payment input—generated repeated evidence across personas.

It was weakest on behavior requiring a precise state transition, boundary condition, or semantic comparison. L3 recall was 20%: only `f-validation-wipes-payment` was found among five reachable flow faults. Actors missed the last-unit boundary, empty-checkout guard, account form reset, and misrouted continuation. They also missed dead controls such as inert sort when their journey did not force an observable before/after check.

Four faults survived both arms: `f-oos-says-in-stock`, `f-receipt-eta-wrong`, `f-continue-shopping-loop`, and `f-cart-continue-removed`. The first two show that merely visiting the right surface is insufficient. An actor reached the out-of-stock product and complained about the dead end, but failed to compare “In stock” with the disabled “Out of stock” button. Another quoted the shipped receipt’s “arrives in 3–5 days” text but reframed the problem as missing tracking information. The flow and navigation misses show the complementary weakness: actors often choose a workable path without testing whether every recovery link leads to the promised destination.

The level result is not evidence that absence is universally easy. Three highly central missing capabilities were strongly cued by persona goals; the missed L4 fault `f-cart-continue-removed` was a secondary recovery link. A better conclusion is that *goal-cued absence* is easy, while optional navigation absence and stateful flow defects are hard. Level labels alone do not predict detection; story pressure and the need for explicit comparison do.

No number of repeated runs under unchanged coverage makes “clean” mean fault-free. The baseline repeated the same 13 faults in both rounds and added none, while known misses remained. More samples reduce stochastic misses only when a story has a nonzero chance of exercising and recognizing the fault. They cannot cure a zero-probability blind spot. Operationally, require at least two consecutive rounds with zero seeded/real findings, zero emergent findings, and all pinned regressions green; in this study that makes both arms three-round climbs. Even then, label the result “clean under this suite,” and add targeted assertions for every high-risk state transition rather than claiming seamlessness.

## Instrument recommendations

1. **Use discovery for breadth once, then shift spend to forced journeys.** The initial discovery panel found 13 reachable faults, but later generic rounds generated almost no new seeded signal. Pinned journeys earned their cost by verifying each repair and by exposing `f-empty-cart-jargon` after an empty-checkout fix unmasked it. Add explicit compare-before/after journeys for sort, clear-search, continue-shopping, account validation retention, exact-stock purchase, empty checkout, receipt ETA, and contradictory stock copy.

2. **Run a three-person panel, not all four by default.** Either cautious-first-timer + returning-regular + weekend-browser or gift-rusher + returning-regular + weekend-browser reproduced all 14 detected faults in the accumulated matrix. Returning-regular uniquely supplied order-history coverage; weekend-browser uniquely supplied the empty-cart and breadcrumb detections. Cautious-first-timer had the highest solo yield (10), while gift-rusher added no unique fault to the full panel. Keep gift-rusher as a targeted stress persona for rapid repeat-click and checkout behavior, not a mandatory general-discovery seat.

3. **Escalate actor tier only to settle a named ambiguity.** The one expensive-actor probe clarified that trust/policy concerns were real to a shopper but outside the frozen specification. That is useful adjudication, not evidence of improved seeded recall. The default mini actor produced the study’s substantive detection set. There is no evidence here to recommend a higher default actor or any different grader tier; run a replicated tier factorial before making H5-style cost claims.

4. **Treat probes as decision tools, not detection runs.** Policy’s two probes found no seeded fault. One prevented an out-of-scope trust-content fix; the other motivated B17, an above-clean improvement. Spend a probe when its possible outcomes change the fix decision. Otherwise, a deterministic regression story or source-level assertion is cheaper and easier to interpret.

5. **Deduplicate before humans or fixers see the report, and calibrate recurring noise by class.** Baseline nonduplicate precision was only 47.5% and 51.1%. Once seeded faults were mostly removed, each arm still emitted roughly 180–197 nonduplicate false positives per round (158 in naive round 1), with about 200 raw findings. Recurrent below-fold, visual-prominence, out-of-spec comparison/trust content, and the 20-second toast latency artifact should be tagged or suppressed automatically. Multi-persona agreement must raise priority only after the class passes the specification and clean-reference filters; agreement alone also amplified false themes.

6. **Fix the stop rule and the harness.** A clean round must count emergent defects and unresolved real issue-classes, not only seeded true positives. A transient control should remain actionable for the actor’s measured step latency, or the harness should detect expiry and distinguish it from app failure. Finally, report three separate quantities: faults detected by the actor, faults repaired through fixer inspection, and faults still live. Combining them turns a repair system’s useful code reasoning into overstated test recall.

The practitioner’s trust posture should therefore be asymmetric: trust Playtest to discover high-salience journey failures and to generate reproducible evidence; do not trust its silence on edge conditions, recovery navigation, semantic contradictions, or previously unforced states. Its best role is an exploratory layer feeding deterministic regressions and human/spec adjudication—not the final oracle that a storefront is seamless.
